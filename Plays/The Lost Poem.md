## Characters
Mr. L.  
Mr. T.

(A studio in The Tribune building. The walls are adorned with two calendars, a pastel by Neysa Moran McMein and a cobweb. The floors are polished save in two 3x7 rectangular spaces, the former home of two rugs. The studio is furnished with a typewriter desk, three chairs, a waste-basket, a gobboon and a lavatory with running hot and cold water. On the desk are sheets of copy paper and a library consisting of three cerise and one field gray telephone books. The door to the studio has been left unlocked in expectation of a visit from Mr. Clean Towels. Mr. L. is seated at the desk, about to tear off a poem. The door opens and Mr. T. enters.)

MR. T.  
No sign on the door, so I just walked in. My name is T⸺. They sent me up here from downstairs. (Advances and stands beside the desk.) I tear off some articles once in awhile myself. I'm what you might call an unknown writing man. (He removes his overcoat and lays it over a chair.) I was wondering how I could sell my articles.

MR. L.  
I was wondering the same thing.

MR. T.  
I thought you might know some syndicate.

MR. L.  
There's one right downstairs where you came from.

MR. T.  
Yes, I saw them, but they said they had all they wanted. You see, I write along the lines of George Fitch. Here's some of my articles. I tore off these three in an hour yesterday, over at the Public Library. (He hands over three tear-offs.)

(Mr. L. reads the first two. The first is about different diseases treated at Rochester, Minn. The second is called "Cabarets" and is rather risky, as they say. Mr. L. is too exhausted to tackle No. 3.)

MR. T.  
Do you think there'd be a market for those in a syndicate. Is that second one too raw?

MR. L.  
Well, you know syndicated stuff goes to small towns as well as large ones. So if I were you, I'd cut out th elegs.

MR. T.  
Call them limbs?

MR. L.  
No, amputate them entierly.

MR. T.  
Ha, ha, ha.

MR. L.  
Thank you.

MR. T.  
Well, now, I lived out west during George Fitch's life and I used to read his articles and other humorists of the past and so on and I found out the salary they were getting and honestly I was astounded. SO I figured, why not get into that game myself?

MR. L.  
I guess they used to pay pretty fair money.

---

MR. T.  
Do you know any syndicates in New York?

MR. L.  
(Writes down names and addresses of a few.)

MR. T.  
(Takes list.) Which ones would you advise me to go to first?

MR. L.  
I'd take them in alphabetical order.

MR. T.  
I've written a novel, too; 125,000 words.

MR. L.  
I didn't know there were that many.

MR. T.  
It's in my suitcase.

MR. L.  
(Breathes heavily.)

MR. T.  
Well, I hope I haven't taken your time.

MR. L.  
No, indeed. But if I were you I'd go right back downstairs to our own syndicate and talk to them again.

MR. T.  
Well, maybe I will. (He goes out.)

MR. L.  
(Locks door and decides not to tear off poem.)
