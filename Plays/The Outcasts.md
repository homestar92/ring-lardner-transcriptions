("There was one New York critic, for example, who lamented because, in taking a Long Island house party as the setting for 'Upstairs and Down,' we had—according to him—chosen a cross section of life with which we were not familiar. The critic in question happened to have been a baseball reporter before he became a dramatic reviewer, so possibly his own experience of Long Island house parties had been limited."—The Hattons.)

## Characters
Brown, a New York baseball reporter.  
Mrs. Brown, his wife.  
Six or Seven Brownies.

---

(Scene—The Browns' one room apartment on the lower east side of New York. The walls are dirty and bare, save in a few places where fragments of cheap wall-paper still hang. Near the wall at left, a one-burner gas plate, a few kitchen utensils, a battered dinner pail, a rickety kitchen table and two broken chairs. In the center at the rear, a door. Near it, an old stand on which are a cracked wash-basin and a pitcher. On a worn-out mattress in the center near the front, Brown is snoring loudly. To the right, six or seven babies are lying on the floor loudly quarreling over possession of the "bed-clothing," a frayed overcoat. Mrs. Brown, in rags, is lighting the gas burner.)

MRS. BROWN  
(goes over near the mattress at center) Brown, it's four o'clock. Ain't you never goin' to git up?

BROWN  
(stops snoring) Shut your mouth, d⸺n you! Can't a man have peace in their own house!

MRS. BROWN  
(hysterically) House! You call this hole a house!

BROWN  
Well, it's all the house we got, ain't it? (Half rises.) And you knowed what I was when you married me. (Sits on edge of mattress. He is dressed in ragged red underwear and yellowish socks.)

MRS. BROWN  
Yes, but you promised you'd quit reportin' baseball.

BROWN  
Uh-huh, and then we'd be like some o' the other ex-baseball writers—Granville Rice and Irving Koob—prob'ly sleepin in the streets.

---

MRS. BROWN  
But you said you was goin' to git on the police force, so's we could horn into some o' them dances down to the Pastry Cooks' Hall. (She goes over to the gas plate, takes a small tin pan off it and places the pan on the table.)

BROWN  
(pulls on a pair of overalls and congress shoes) There you go again with your yelpin' about Society! Do you s'pose I feel like dancin' when I've slaved from five in the mornin' till ten at night, reportin' ball games at two bits a day! (He gets up and walks toward the table at left.)

MRS. BROWN  
Ain't you goin' to wash this mornin'. This is Tuesday.

BROWN  
I'll wash when I need it! Well, what is they to eat? A old banana, I s'pose.

MRS. BROWN  
No, I've stewed a prune for you.

BROWN  
One prune? Where's that other one?

---

MRS. BROWN  
I'm savin' it for the childer.

BROWN  
A whole prune for them? How many is they, for the Lor's sakes?

MRS. BROWN  
They was six last night. I ain't counted since then.

BROWN  
(Sits in one of the broken chairs and gulps down the prune. Gets up and starts toward the door at the rear.)

MRS. BROWN  
(holding out the dinner pail) Here's your dinner: a soda cracker.

BROWN  
(turns and grabs the pail) I wonder where I left that butts. Well, I can't wait all day to look for it. They's a double-header in Brooklyn today and it's a long walk.

MRS. BROWN  
(excitedly) In Brooklyn! Oh, Brown, take me with you oncet!

BROWN  
Have you went nuts? What about the kids?

MRS. BROWN  
I don't care! I must go to Brooklyn! O, just think! Long Island! I might see some of the dramatic critics and playwrights on their way to Coney! O, take me with you! (Grasps his arm.)

BROWN  
(Frees himself) Let me go, d⸺n you!

MRS. BROWN  
(Throws her arms around his knees) Not alone! I'll go to Brooklyn or I'll go to he⸺ll!

BROWN  
(Draws his scoring pencil and stabs her to the heart) Well, you won't go to Brooklyn.
